# Rehabilitation Engineering Framework for Adaptive Sport Participation Following T7-T8 Paraplegia

**Abstract**—This paper presents a rehabilitation engineering analysis for a 27-year-old woman with complete T7-T8 paraplegia following a competitive ski racing accident, whose goals include participation in adaptive mountain biking and alpine skiing. Using the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework, we characterize her physiological profile and identify a rehabilitation engineering team. Four candidate solutions are proposed and compared: functional electrical stimulation (FES), epidural spinal stimulation (ESS), a robotic lower-limb exoskeleton (ReWalk Personal Exoskeleton), and an adaptive mountain bike (aMTB). Solutions are evaluated across functional, economic, regulatory, and ethical dimensions. An assessment protocol is presented. Results are expected to demonstrate meaningful improvement in capacity, performance, and participation.

## Introduction

The subject of this paper (hereafter "the user") is a 27-year-old female and former competitive ski racer who sustained a traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) at the T7-T8 vertebral level three years ago following a high-speed ski racing collision. The user experiences no motor or sensory function below the level of injury. Structurally, the user presents with permanent bilateral lower extremity paralysis, complete absence of proprioception below the T8 dermatome, and neurogenic bladder and bowel dysfunction. Secondary structural risks include disuse-related muscle atrophy, osteoporosis, and pressure injury from immobility.

In regards to activities, she is a manual wheelchair user who independently carries out daily living activities from a seated position including self-care and driving. She is unable to perform any lower-extremety dependent physical activity without assistive technology.

In regards to participation, she experiences restriction from the recreational and sporting activities that formed a central part of her identity and life prior to injury. She reports a strong desire to return to competitive and recreational adaptive sport, specifically mountain biking and alpine skiing.

The user lives with her husband in an accessible suburban home near Burlington, Vermont proximal to outdoor recreation areas. Environmental barriers include limited local access to spinal cord injury (SCI) rehabilitation resources and variable insurance coverage for advanced assistive technologies. Facilitators include young age, high motivation, and prior background in athletic training.

### Stakeholders

The clinical team for this patient includes a physical therapist, an occupational therapist, a rehabilitation engineer, a urologist for bladder dysfunction, and a primary care doctor. Given her fitness goals, an adaptive sports coach is included as well. Personal stakeholders include her husband who serves as a primary caregiver, as well as her former college ski racing teammates as her social community. Her health insurance provider is a stakeholder as reimbursement will constrain the feasible technological solutions. Device manufacturers are technical stakeholders for any rehabilitation technologies considered.

### Prior and current interventions

Following injury and surgery, the user completed an inpatient rehabilitation program focused on wheelchair mobility and received education on managing secondary complications. She has been following a home exercise program focused on upper extremity strength and mobility. No functional restoration interventions have been prescribed, and no adaptive sport program has been initiated. The current standard of care for complete T7-T8 paraplegia provides no pathway to recovering lower extremity motor function through conventional rehabilitation methods. In this paper, we compare four rehabilitation engineering solutions. For functional restoration, we consider functional electrical stimulation (FES) and epidural spinal stimulation (ESS). For functional replacement, we consider a robotic lower limb exoskeleton (ReWalk Personal Exoskeleton) and adaptive mountain bike (aMTB).

## Methods

### Team

The rehabilitation engineering team was created around the user's structural and functional impairments and stated goals. Clinically, the user requires care from a urologist for neurogenic bladder dysfunction. The user's functional goals include mobility and sports participation, which are best addressed by a physical therapist, occupational therapist, and adaptive sports coach. A rehabilitation engineer would take the lead on device selection and customization, in collaboration with relevant device manufacturers to advise on technical specifications and maintenance. As the primary caretaker, the user's husband is a personal stakeholder and may help facilitate goals. Health insurance is a financial stakeholder as reimbursement constraints will define feasible solutions.

## Candidate Solutions

Four candidate solutions were identified to address the user's primary functional limitation, complete bilateral paralysis of lower extremity, and goals to participate in sport. Two functional restoration solutions (FES and ESS) and two functional replacement solutions (ReWalk Personal Exoskeleton and aMTB) were considered. Solutions were compared using the following criteria: independent mobility, alignment with sport participation goals, versatility on variable terrain types, ease of donning/doffing, maintenance and fixability, insurance reimbursement likelihood, financial cost of device, metabolic cost of using device, and learning curve. Each solution was scored with a +1, 0, or -1 relative to the reference condition (manual wheelchair), and scores were used to rank candidates.

### Functional electrical stimulation (FES)

FES delivers electrical impulses to peripheral motor nerves through surface or implanted electrodes to generate contractions in paralyzed muscles of the lower extremity. FES-assisted cycling is the modality considered in this case and activates the muscles respective to a motorized crank. FES targets the rehabilitation principles of use-dependent neuroplasticity and musculoskeletal adaptation. Repeated muscle activation counteracts disuse atrophy and slows bone density loss.

### Epidural spinal stimulation (ESS)

ESS involves surgical implantation of a multicontact electrode array on the posterior epidural surface of the spinal cord. Continuous or patterned stimulation enables lower extremity voluntary motor output. ESS has been shown to have restoration of volitional movement in individuals with motor-complete SCI [1], including improvements in cardiovascular, bladder, and bowel function. ESS parameters are highly individualized and require extensive trial and error to program [3]. This makes reliable and generalizable programming of ESS systems difficult in practice.

### Robotic lower limb exoskeleton (ReWalk)

The ReWalk is an FDA approved powered lower limb exoskeleton with motorized hip and knee joints. It enables ambulation with bilateral forearm crutches. The goal of the device is to enable people with paraplegia due to SCI to ambulate independently to carry out functions of daily life. In a clinical study of 12 subjects with thoracic-level motor-complete SCI, all subjects were able to independently and continuously walk for at least 50–100 m with velocities ranging from 0.03–0.45 m/s [2]. Design parameters include hip and knee joint torque, step length, step height, and walking speed.

### Adaptive mountain bike (aMTB)

The adaptive mountain bike is a recumbent hand-cycle for unpaved terrain. It directly addresses the user's goal of participating in adaptive sports without directly requiring function of lower extremities. Adjustable parameters include seat angle and handlebar position which influence comfort, stability, and efficiency.

### Selection framework

Candidate solutions were evaluated using a weighted scoring matrix. Criteria were determined based on relevance to the user's stated goals and functional profile. The manual wheelchair is used as a reference; candidate solutions are compared against it. Total scores across criteria determined ranking, with the highest-scoring solution selected as the best candidate to test first.

### Personalization and Optimization framework

We use two variables to personalize aMTB setup, trunk stability and upper extremity joint ROM. Trunk stability is measured using the Trunk Control Test (TCT) [5]. A lower score is an indication of greater need for seat recline to maintain stability on rough terrain. Upper extremity joint ROM is measured using a goniometer at the shoulder, elbow, and wrist to determine the optimal handlebar/crank positions to maximize force generation and minimize injury risk.

We construct a digital twin of the user and aMTB, considering body geometry, trunk stability profile, and joint ROM. The model simulates trail features including bumps, slope grades, and turns across a range of seat angles and handlebar positions. Results show trunk displacement and joint loading. On the bike, the following sensors are used to measure data: a heart rate monitor and power meter are used to calculate cardiovascular effort, accelerometer on frame to measure vibration, and IMUs on the trunk to measure movement.

### Assessment

Capacity is assessed by conducting a 6-minute ride test, on a standardized flat course. Outcomes measured include distance traveled, heart rate, and the user's rate of perceived exertion (RPE) on a scale of 1-10. Threshold: no pain or extreme fatigue reported, and at least 10% greater distance covered than with generic bike setup.

Performance is assessed using wearable sensors attached to the bike and worn on the body while riding on a public trail network. Outcomes are measured and analyzed on specific segments as defined by users of the Strava app. Measurements include distance traveled, speed, heart rate, the user's RPE, and number of falls. Threshold: 10% faster and less falls than a generic bike setup.

Participation is assessed through a structured interview. The user will be asked about their weekly time spent on the bike and self-reported enjoyment. Threshold: at least 60 minutes of trail riding per week and positive enjoyment rating.

### Hypothetical Outcomes Results

Following an 8-week protocol, assessments are performed to analyze capacity, performance, and participation.

Capacity (6-Minute Ride Test): The user travels 1320 m with the personalized aMTB setup versus 1180 m with the generic setup, an 11.9% improvement exceeding the 10% threshold. Average heart rate is 148 bpm; RPE is 8.5. No pain reported.

Performance (as seen in Strava data from 45-minute activity in Bolton, VT and sensors): average time on analyzed segment shows significant decrease from previous rides, 13% faster than slowest time. Zero falls recorded with personalized setup versus two falls with generic setup.

Participation (structured interview at week 8): User reports 75 minutes per week of trail riding, exceeding the 60-minute threshold. She rates enjoyment 8.5/10 and reports meaningful restoration of athletic identity.

## Discussion

The subject discussed is a 27-year-old female with complete T7-T8 traumatic paraplegia whose primary goals are to participate in adaptive sports, specifically mountain biking and alpine skiing. Four candidate solutions of functional restoration and replacement were proposed, compared, and evaluated. The aMTB was selected as the best solution for the user's stated goals. A personalization framework using an AI model and digital twin was developed to configure the device in a way that optimizes capacity, performance, and participation.

Because the user presented with complete lower extremity paralysis, we focused on solutions not requiring lower extremity function. The aMTB was biomechanically well-suited due to her upper extremity strength and prior athletic conditioning background. Reduced trunk stability as a functional impairment due to injury informed the seat angle parameter.

Regular aMTB use is expected to improve her cardiovascular fitness, muscular endurance of upper extremity, and psychosocial wellbeing. The device does not directly address lower extremity motor function or neurogenic bladder dysfunction. At the activity level, the aMTB restores independent mobility on variable terrain. The user is able to navigate environments that are not accessible by manual wheelchair. At the participation level, the user is able to engage in adaptive mountain biking which also benefits her social life.

Economically, adaptive mountain bikes range from $3k–10k+, and insurance coverage for advanced recreational adaptive devices is often denied. Mitigation strategies include engaging the health insurance provider early on with justification indicating functional necessity rather than sport. Adaptive sport grants may also be available. The aMTB is not subject to FDA medical device regulation which reduces regulatory barriers. Other regulatory standards that may be relevant include RESNA ANSI WC-1:2019 which regulates mechanical testing for wheeled mobility devices and ISO 7176-15 for information disclosure and labeling. Ethically, the primary concern is equity of access; advanced adaptive sport technology disproportionately benefits individuals with financial resources.

Additional tools that may enhance outcomes beyond the proposed solution include FES and a trunk orthosis. FES may help the user activate lower extremity muscles to reduce atrophy. A trunk orthosis, active or passive, may help the user improve trunk stabilization for riding on rough terrain.

When prompted with the case profile and goals, a LLM (ChatGPT, OpenAI) recommended FES as the top solution. Other suggested solutions include an adaptive mountain bike with dynamic suspension, trunk stabilization orthosis, and advanced sport wheelchair. The LLM appropriately identified the adaptive sporting equipment (aMTB) as supporting the user's goal to return to sport but weighed FES more heavily citing that it is a more useful long-term implement that may also be helpful in other life aspects beyond fitness goals. When asked to develop an assessment protocol, the LLM proposed taking quantitative measurements of VO2 max, bone density, and power output, for each of the domains, capacity, performance, and participation. The LLM differentiated the three assessments using time periods with capacity being measured at week 0, performance being quantified using measurements taken over the course of several weeks, and participation using measurements taken over several months.
